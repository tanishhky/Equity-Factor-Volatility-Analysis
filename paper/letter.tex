\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Volatility-managed factors look strong in spanning regressions, but those regressions describe a portfolio no investor could have formed in advance. We rebuild the Fama-French five factors and momentum so that each month's exposure uses only prior data, is capped at 1.5 times and pays trading costs, and we estimate variance from every daily return with a one-month half-life, a learning rate chosen on pre-2017 data from 24 variants in a pre-registered comparison. From \RTstart{} to \RTend{}, only momentum improves significantly, from \MomOrig{} to \MomNet{}. After the published samples end, the canonical prior-month rule adds nothing to momentum, while the daily-data rule keeps part of the gain at half the turnover; over 116 months neither difference is statistically significant. Choosing the learning rate by recent performance does worse than fixing it, and no real-time combination beats its original factor after costs. An exact decomposition of the Sharpe ratio into a return channel and a risk channel explains the pattern.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} volatility-managed portfolios, factor timing, momentum, learning rate, out-of-sample performance

\noindent\textbf{JEL:} G11, G12, G14
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\citet{moreira2017} show that scaling a factor's exposure by the inverse of its recent variance raises the Sharpe ratio of many equity factors. Their evidence is a spanning regression of the managed factor on the original. A positive intercept means some mix of the two beats the original, but the mix uses full-sample moments no investor had. \citet{cederburg2020} make the point directly: across 103 strategies ending in December 2016, real-time combinations of managed and original portfolios usually fail to beat the original. \citet{liu2019} show that the market-timing version relies on a scale constant fitted with future data, and \citet{barroso2021} that transaction costs erase most of the gains, with the market and momentum partly robust. Momentum is the most consistent survivor, in line with the crash evidence of \citet{barroso2015} and \citet{daniel2016}.

This letter asks three questions. Which of the six most widely used equity factors improve once every position uses only past data, leverage is capped and costs are paid? What happened after the published samples ended? Moreira and Muir's data end in 2015 and Cederburg et al.'s in 2016, so January 2017 to \RTend{} is out of sample for both, and \citet{mclean2016} find that anomaly returns are 58\% lower after publication. And does the rule stop adapting? Its estimators learn at fixed or vanishing rates; we test whether other learning rates keep it working.

The answers are narrow. Only momentum's managed version beats the original over the full sample. After the published samples end, the canonical rule, which scales by the prior month's realized variance, loses momentum's gain, while a rule that uses every daily return with a one-month half-life keeps part of it at half the turnover; neither difference is statistically significant over a decade. Profitability, whose managed version earns a significant spanning alpha in \citet{cederburg2020}, shows no significant real-time gain. An exact decomposition into a return channel and a risk channel explains the pattern.

\section{Data and method}

We use the market, size (SMB), value (HML), profitability (RMW), investment (CMA) and momentum factors of \citet{fama2015} and \citet{carhart1997} from the Kenneth R. French Data Library, July 1963 to \RTend{}. Monthly returns $f_m$ come from the monthly files; daily returns $r_d$ serve only to measure risk. The canonical signal of \citet{moreira2017} is the prior month's realized variance,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RV}_m=\sum_{d\in m}\left(r_d-\bar r_m\right)^2 ,
\end{equation}
where $\bar r_m$ is the mean daily return in month $m$. Our main signal uses every daily return with an exponential learning rate:
\begin{equation}
\hat\sigma^2_d=\lambda\,\hat\sigma^2_{d-1}+(1-\lambda)\,r_d^2,\qquad \lambda=2^{-1/21},\qquad S_m=21\,\hat\sigma^2_{d(m)} ,
\label{eq:ewma}
\end{equation}
where $d(m)$ is the last trading day of month $m$. A 21-day half-life puts about half the weight on the latest month and keeps some memory of earlier ones. It is one of the 24 variants of Section~\ref{sec:learning}, chosen for momentum on 1973 to 2016 data under a plan committed before any variant was run; we apply it to all six factors so that the paper reports one rule, and report the canonical rule, $S_m=\mathrm{RV}_m$, alongside. French's daily momentum factor is re-formed daily, so for momentum both signals proxy the variance of the monthly-formed factor. The data are today's vintage, which French revises as CRSP and Compustat update, not the returns as first published.

\paragraph{Managed factor.} The exposure held in month $m$ is set at the end of month $m-1$ (Figure~\ref{fig:design}):
\begin{equation}
w_m=\min\!\left(\frac{c_{m-1}}{S_{m-1}},\,L\right),\qquad
c_{m-1}=\frac{\operatorname{sd}\left(f_1,\dots,f_{m-1}\right)}{\operatorname{sd}\left(f_s/S_{s-1}:\ s\le m-1\right)} .
\end{equation}
The scale $c_{m-1}$ is the expanding-window version of Moreira and Muir's full-sample constant: it makes the uncapped managed factor as volatile as the original over the data seen so far. The first 120 months are a burn-in, so positions start in \RTstart{} (\RTmonths{} months). The cap is $L=1.5$, the higher of the two caps (1 and 1.5) in Moreira and Muir's Table IV. Changing exposure costs $\kappa=14$ basis points per unit, the highest of their cost levels, so the managed return is
\begin{equation}
f^{\sigma}_m=w_m f_m-\kappa\left|w_m-w_{m-1}\right| .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Spanning regression.} $f^{\sigma}_m=\alpha+\beta f_m+\varepsilon_m$, with Newey-West $t$-statistics (six lags). Because $f^\sigma$ is net of costs, $\alpha$ is the return a static position in the original could not replicate, after costs.

\paragraph{Real-time combination.} Following \citet{cederburg2020}, from month 60 of the real-time sample the investor forms the mean-variance mix of the original and managed factor using the expanding window $s<m$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix}a_m\\ b_m\end{pmatrix}=\hat\Sigma_{m-1}^{-1}\hat\mu_{m-1},\qquad
e_m=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(k_m\,(a_m+b_m w_m),\,-L,\,L\right),
\end{equation}
where $\hat\mu$ and $\hat\Sigma$ are the mean and covariance of $(f_s,f^\sigma_s)$ and $k_m$ scales the mix to the original factor's volatility. Since $f^\sigma=w f$ before costs, the mix is a single exposure $e_m$ to the original factor, capped like the managed factor and charged the same cost: $f^{c}_m=e_m f_m-\kappa|e_m-e_{m-1}|$.

\paragraph{Inference.} For two return series with sample moments $\hat v=(\hat\mu_1,\hat\mu_2,\hat\gamma_1,\hat\gamma_2)$, where $\hat\gamma_i$ is the mean of squared returns, the Sharpe difference is
\begin{equation}
\hat\Delta=g(\hat v)=\frac{\hat\mu_1}{\sqrt{\hat\gamma_1-\hat\mu_1^2}}-\frac{\hat\mu_2}{\sqrt{\hat\gamma_2-\hat\mu_2^2}},\qquad
\operatorname{se}(\hat\Delta)=\sqrt{\nabla g(\hat v)^{\top}\hat\Psi\,\nabla g(\hat v)/T} ,
\end{equation}
with $\hat\Psi$ a Bartlett long-run covariance of the four moment series, with lag length equal to the bootstrap block length. We test $\Delta=0$ with the studentized circular block bootstrap of \citet{ledoit2008}: 4,999 resamples of 12-month blocks, each with its own block-based standard error $\operatorname{se}^*$. The $p$-value is $\big(1+\#\{|\hat\Delta^*-\hat\Delta|/\operatorname{se}^*\ge|\hat\Delta|/\operatorname{se}\}\big)/5000$, and the 95\% interval is $\hat\Delta\pm q^*_{0.95}\operatorname{se}$, where $q^*_{0.95}$ is the 95th percentile of the resampled statistic. Within each family of six factors, $p$-values are adjusted with the step-down procedure of \citet{holm1979}: with ordered $p_{(1)}\le\dots\le p_{(6)}$, the adjusted value is $\tilde p_{(j)}=\max_{i\le j}\min\{1,(7-i)\,p_{(i)}\}$. A test that the Sharpe gain differs across two periods treats the periods as independent and combines their bootstrap draws in the same studentized way.

\paragraph{Why timing can help or hurt.} For any exposure series, the mean managed return decomposes exactly as
\begin{equation}
\overline{f^\sigma}=\bar w\,\bar f+\widehat{\operatorname{cov}}(w_m,f_m)-\kappa\,\overline{|\Delta w|} ,
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
with the covariance taken over the same months. The first term is what a constant exposure $\bar w$ would earn, and a constant exposure has the original factor's Sharpe ratio. Dividing the managed Sharpe ratio by the original's therefore gives, whenever $\bar f>0$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{SR}(f^\sigma)}{\mathrm{SR}(f)}=
\underbrace{\left(1+\frac{\widehat{\operatorname{cov}}(w_m,f_m)-\kappa\,\overline{|\Delta w|}}{\bar w\,\bar f}\right)}_{M:\ \text{return channel}}
\times
\underbrace{\frac{\bar w\,\operatorname{sd}(f)}{\operatorname{sd}(f^\sigma)}}_{R:\ \text{risk channel}} .
\label{eq:channels}
\end{equation}
$M$ measures the return that timing adds, net of costs, relative to a constant exposure of the same average size; $R$ measures how much volatility timing removes relative to that exposure. \citet{moreira2017} motivate $w\propto1/\sigma^2$ with the mean-variance weight $w^*_m=\mu_m/(\gamma\sigma^2_m)$. If the expected return $\mu_m$ does not vary with variance, the covariance is zero in expectation and the whole gain comes through $R$. If $\mu_m$ is lower in turbulent months, as in the momentum crashes of \citet{daniel2016}, the covariance adds a second gain through $M$. If $\mu_m$ is higher in turbulent months, $M$ falls below one and the rule scales down exactly when the factor pays.

\section{Learning rates}
\label{sec:learning}

The rule has three estimators, each with an implicit learning rate. The canonical signal weights the last month's days equally and ignores earlier ones; the scale constant $c$ and the combination weights use expanding windows, whose step size $1/t$ has fallen to about 1/640 by 2017, so they have effectively stopped learning. After the canonical rule's post-2016 results were known, and before any alternative was run, we committed a plan (\texttt{paper/PLAN\_adaptive.md} in the code repository) that fixes 24 variants: six signals (S0, prior-month realized variance; S1 to S3, exponentially weighted daily variance with half-lives of 5, 21 and 63 trading days; S4, a forecast in the spirit of \citet{corsi2009} from the last day, week and month; S5, realized variance of the last 13 weekly returns), four scale constants (B0, expanding; B1 to B3, half-lives of 3, 5 and 10 years) and two exponentially weighted combinations. Because the post-2016 results were already known, no rule uses them to pick a variant: the best variant on 1973 to 2016, evaluated once afterwards; a real-time rule holding each month the variant with the best trailing 10-year Sharpe ratio; and the superior-predictive-ability test of \citet{hansen2005} against the canonical rule, with loss differentials $r_{k,m}/\operatorname{sd}(r_k)-r_{0,m}/\operatorname{sd}(r_0)$, whose mean is the monthly Sharpe difference. The pre-committed adoption rule made the pre-2017 selection for momentum the main specification if either of the last two showed a significant improvement for momentum with the same sign after 2016.

That met the adoption rule, at the margin. The real-time rule points the other way: chasing the recently best learning rate switched variant \AdMomSwitches{} times and earned \AdMomRuleTwo{} against \AdMomRuleTwoCur{} for the canonical rule over the same months ($p=\AdMomRuleTwoP{}$). The learning rate that worked best recently is not the one that works next.

Figure~\ref{fig:grid} shows three further patterns. The fastest signal, a 5-day half-life, trails the canonical rule before 2017 for \AdSOneWorsePre{} of the six factors, but helps in a crash. At the end of February 2020 it had cut market exposure to \AdMktFebFast{}, against \AdMktFebCur{} for the canonical rule, and its net return over February to April was \AdMktCovidFast\% instead of \AdMktCovidCur\% (Figure~\ref{fig:stress}), although no signal held more than \AdMktAprMax{} of the market for the April rebound. Weekly realized variance over a quarter is the best momentum variant after 2016 (\AdMomPostSFive{}) but among the worst before (\AdMomPreSFive{}). With the canonical signal, a 3-year half-life for the scale constant lowers momentum's pre-2017 Sharpe ratio to \AdMomPreBOne{} but raises its post-2016 one to \AdMomPostBOne{}. An adaptive scale constant helps value most (column B1 in Figure~\ref{fig:grid}): its pre-2017 choice, \AdHmlRuleOne{}, cut value's post-2016 loss against the canonical rule (Sharpe ratio \AdHmlPostSel{} against \AdHmlPostCur{}, $p=\AdHmlPostSelP{}$; Hansen $p=\AdHmlSpaPost{}$), the clearest case for a faster learning rate. Exponentially weighted combination weights do worse than expanding ones for \AdComboWorseCount{} of the six factors. Faster learning trades stability for recent fit, and the trade rarely pays. Faster rebalancing is not tested: every French daily momentum series is re-formed daily, so daily returns of the monthly-formed factor would need CRSP.

\section{Conclusion}

Built with only past data, capped leverage and costs, volatility management improves one of six widely used equity factors, momentum. After the published samples end, the canonical rule stopped adding value to momentum; estimating variance from every daily return with a one-month half-life keeps part of the gain at half the turnover. A decade is too short to show that this, or any learning rate, is reliably better, and choosing the learning rate by recent performance is worse than fixing it. No real-time combination beat its original factor after costs. Equation (\ref{eq:channels}) gives investors a check before adopting the rule: it pays only when the product of the return and risk channels exceeds one.

Two limits apply. The post-2016 window has \PostMonths{} months, enough to detect a large change but not a moderate one. We charge costs only on changes in exposure, not on the trading inside each factor portfolio, so every net figure is an upper bound.
